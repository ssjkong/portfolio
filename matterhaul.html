Problem

The construction supply industry relies on phone calls, paper, and knowing the right person.

While this system has held up for a long time, it's slow and shops lose potential jobs when they can't respond quickly.

Matterhaul is building the AI-native operating system for the built world — helping distributors and their partners replace disconnected, manual processes with a connected, intelligent platform. I joined as a freelance designer to help shape a key early piece of that product: the moment a salesperson turns a customer's need into a quote.

Process
Discover

Research

I partnered with the founding team to run research sessions with local construction shops, from lumberyards to high-end kitchen and bath showrooms. Each shop had deep domain expertise, its own vocabulary, and its own version of the same underlying mess. We sat down with salespeople and managers to observe and understand their workflow.

On-site research at construction supplier location in SF, CA

What was clear was how much institutional knowledge was needed to do the job, and how that system didn't scale well. One shop estimated they missed roughly 2 out of 3 potential jobs simply because no one was available to pick up the phone. They walked us through their current setup: a calculator app pulled up next to their quoting tool, manually marking up prices line by line. Someone could walk in with a request for a kitchen remodel and a fast, experienced salesperson would turn it over in a few days, while a more junior person would take over a week. Another shop walked us through the headaches of tracking lead times and legal responsibility when things go wrong — details that kept slipping through the cracks of a half-digital, half-paper system.

They showed us the calculator app sitting open next to their quoting tool — manually marking up prices, line by line.

The problem wasn't their expertise, it was the outdated tools they used to manage the work. The opportunity to make a real difference here was enormous.

Define

At the end of the day, these shops want to do one thing well: get accurate orders delivered to their customers, fast. That means being responsive, technically sharp, and easy to work with. Every hour spent fighting a slow tool is an hour not spent winning the next job.

We mapped the full pipeline — first contact, quote, sign-off, delivery — and decided to focus on the front end: quote generation.

A faster quote could be the difference between winning or losing the job.

The broader vision for this phase of the process was an AI-powered end-to-end workflow:

  • A voice agent to capture phone orders so nothing falls through the cracks
  • A paper-to-PDF flow for converting handwritten or legacy quotes
  • An AI communications layer for ongoing customer updates
  • And at the center of it all — the Quote Builder, the human-facing workspace where salespeople could actually do their job

Designing the Quote Builder also meant designing the place where human expertise and AI infrastructure meet, which felt like the most important design problem to get right.

Develop

I designed the Quote Builder, starting with wireframes and working closely with engineers to build the tool, followed by tight feedback and iteration loops with real users.

The engineers had done serious backend work building a searchable database of brands and products across construction verticals. That was the foundation everything else depended on. If a salesperson typed what they needed and couldn't find it, they would still have to trawl across dozens of websites to find their product.

The main design challenge was density.

Construction quotes are long and messy — lots of product types, varying stock availability, customer-specific pricing. The interface had to carry a lot of weight without feeling heavy. There was plenty of low-hanging fruit early on, but the trickier work was in the details: figuring out exactly how much information to surface at once, and where to get out of the way.

Wireframes — quotes list, new quote, and quote builder

While we wanted to make everything as seamless as possible, some constraints came from the technology. For example, stock availability required a manual data fetch rather than a live sync, which shaped how we handled that part of the flow. We also received some feedback from users:

  • Larger product images — small thumbnails weren't cutting it for people who needed to visually confirm they had the right item
  • Keyboard shortcuts — power users wanted to move fast without touching the mouse
  • Inline quote sharing — the ability to send a quote list directly within an email was a hit
  • A browser extension — one shop asked if they could add products to a quote while browsing a distributor's site. We suggested a browser extension to fit their workflow, and it was well-received

Every round of testing sharpened the product.

Working closely with the engineering team made the iteration loop fast — we'd test, debrief, and be back at it quickly.

Outcome
Beta

We launched a beta in Berkeley, CA.

The Quote Builder is currently in beta with one of the shops we partnered with during the research and iteration phase — an awesome shop called Ashby Lumber with a location in Berkeley, CA.

The early feedback has been genuinely encouraging. Salespeople said it was significantly faster to find and input products than their old tool. Training junior staff has gotten easier too. With a clear, linear flow and everything in one place, there's less to explain and less room for error.

It's much easier to train junior salespeople — everything is in one product and the flow is just straightforward.

There's still work ahead. The next big design challenge will be building a communications view to track the back-and-forth that happens before and after a quote is signed: proposed changes, delivery estimates, customer reminders. That's the layer that ties the Quote Builder into the broader AI platform Matterhaul is building, and it's the kind of problem I love working on.
